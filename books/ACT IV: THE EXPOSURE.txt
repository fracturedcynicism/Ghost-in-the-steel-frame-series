ACT IV: THE EXPOSURE
CHAPTER SEVEN: THE TEA SHOP CONFESSION (2024)
When Ritika emerged from the basement, a man was waiting for her in the corridor. Late fifties, well-dressed, with the particular bearing of someone who'd spent decades navigating institutional power. He introduced himself as Deepak Bhat, the retired IAS officer whose name appeared on the transfer documents from the coaching academy property.
"I thought we might speak," he said. His voice was gentle, measured, the voice of someone delivering difficult news he wished he didn't have to deliver.
They sat in a small tea shop across from the Writers' Building. The tea shop was cramped, the plastic chairs uncomfortable, the tea served in thick glasses that retained heat. Bhat ordered without asking what she wanted—chai, the traditional way. He stirred it slowly, his hands trembling slightly with age or emotion or the particular weight of unburdening.
"You found the satchel," he said. It wasn't a question.
"I found documents."
"Yes. The documents that should have been destroyed in 1947. Arjun Singh had the foresight to preserve them before the official destruction. He understood what the mechanism was going to become."
Bhat pulled out a sealed envelope and placed it on the table between them.
"This is a deposition. Written, dated, witnessed. It describes the operation of the trust. The names of the officers involved. The amounts of money distributed. It describes the decision to allow Vikram Mallik to expand his academy without proper safety precautions. It describes the understanding that three UPSC aspirants would probably drown if they studied during monsoon in an unsanctioned basement."
"You knew?" Ritika asked, though she already knew the answer. "About Anjali, Rohit, Priya? You knew they would probably drown?"
"I knew the risk had been identified. The municipal engineers filed reports in 2019 when the basement was excavated. The risk assessments were done. The water damage was documented. Someone in the mechanism—I don't know who, it doesn't matter—decided that the risk was acceptable. That the money flowing from the academy was more important than the lives of three young people."
Bhat lifted his glass of chai to his lips, but didn't drink. He set it back down. "I've spent thirty years managing this trust, ensuring its continuation. I believed I was maintaining order. I believed the officers receiving payments were excellent administrators being compensated for their discretion. I believed the political contributions were necessary investments in stability. I believed the land transfers were legitimate business operations."
"And now?" Ritika asked.
"Now I understand that I was simply participating in theft. That I was using my position to ensure that the stolen wealth continued flowing. That I was more than complicit. I was essential to the mechanism."
He pulled another document from the envelope—a handwritten letter, dated and signed.
---
I'm writing this at seventy-nine years old, knowing that I will die before accountability reaches me. The mechanism has protected me my entire career. It will continue to protect me because Article 311 safeguards ensure that officers cannot be prosecuted without specific evidence of criminal conduct, and the mechanism is designed to prevent the creation of such evidence.
But I can no longer carry the weight of silence. I'm releasing this deposition knowing that it will change nothing. The mechanism will adapt. It will offer reforms. It will appear responsive while ensuring that nothing fundamental changes. But at least it will exist in the knowledge that someone inside it has articulated what it is.
This is not redemption. Redemption would require me to have acted forty years ago. This is simply the admission of a man who waited too long.
—Deepak Bhat
---
Ritika opened the envelope. The deposition was meticulous. It detailed the trust structure, the property transfers, the specific financial arrangements. It named the officers. It described the revenue allocation—Schedule B, the mysterious line item that had appeared in dozens of documents.
Schedule B was no longer mysterious.
---
SOVEREIGN ADJUSTMENT PROTOCOL: SCHEDULE B
Annual Revenue Allocation (2024)
Administrative settlements for continued cooperation: ₹47,000,000 distributed annually to rotating committee of IAS/IPS officers
Pension supplements for officers maintaining discretion: ₹28,000,000 annually to retired officers ensuring institutional silence
Political contributions to ensure legislative cooperation: ₹62,000,000 distributed to multiple parties ensuring no investigation
Land acquisition programs for approved operators: ₹83,000,000 facilitating transfer of properties to favored businessmen maintaining the mechanism
Total annual extraction: ₹220,000,000
Mechanism lifespan (1947-2024): 77 years
Estimated total wealth transferred: ₹16,940,000,000
---
Ritika felt something collapse inside her. The mechanism had been extracting sixteen billion rupees over seventy-seven years. It had been using that wealth to buy the loyalty of officers, the silence of politicians, the continued operation of a system that nobody wanted to investigate.
"The coaching academy was part of this?" Ritika asked.
"A recent addition. Vikram Mallik applied for the property in 2017. He was approved because he demonstrated appropriate understanding of how the mechanism worked. His academy generates revenue that flows into the trust. The trust compensates him for his cooperative attitude. The mechanism perpetuates itself."
Bhat finished his chai. His hands were shaking more noticeably now. "I've known for thirty years that this mechanism existed. I knew that it was extracting wealth from India. I knew that it was using that wealth to ensure that nobody in power would ever investigate it. And I did nothing."
"Why?" Ritika asked.
"Because I was comfortable. Because the mechanism protected me. Because my family benefited from the arrangement. Because the alternative—stepping forward, exposing the mechanism—would have required me to sacrifice everything, and I wasn't strong enough to do that."
He stood, preparing to leave. His movements were slow, careful, the movements of an old man managing pain. "You have the evidence now. You have the documentation. You have the satchel. What you do with it is your choice. But know that the mechanism has survived seventy-eight years by absorbing attempts to expose it, by transforming resistance into procedure, by ensuring that those who know about it are removed from positions where they can do damage."
"Why tell me this?" Ritika asked.
"Because I'm dying. I'm ninety-two years old. The mechanism has given me ninety-two comfortable years. And I've come to understand that comfort purchased through the suffering of others is a form of death that occurs while you're still alive."

CHAPTER EIGHT: THE PANICKED MEETING (2024)
Ritika didn't publish Bhat's deposition. She didn't publicize Ghosh's research. She understood that those actions would mark her as a permanent threat to the mechanism, someone who would need to be completely removed.
Instead, she compiled everything—the photographs, the documents, the satchel research, Arjun's reflections, Bhat's deposition—and uploaded it to a secure server with an anonymous address. She sent the address to journalists, to academics who studied administrative systems, to student organizations investigating the coaching academy drowning.
She then went back to her apartment and waited.
Within three days, journalists began checking the documents. Academic institutions began analyzing the land records. Student organizations began investigating the coaching academy drowning with new context.
Within a week, the Additional Police Commissioner announced that he was stepping down from his position for "administrative reasons." The statement was terse. No explanation. No acknowledgment of the leak. Just a resignation letter citing health concerns.
Within two weeks, the government announced a new inquiry into coaching academy safety regulations. A committee was formed. Procedures were initiated. The mechanism had begun its response—not denial but adaptation.
But something else happened too. The mechanism panicked.
She heard about the panicked trust meeting from Anjali, who still had sources in the bureaucracy. The managers—retired IAS officers, serving IPS officers, businessmen connected to the network—had gathered in a private room at a hotel in South Delhi to discuss whether the mechanism could survive the exposure.
They met in a suite on the fifteenth floor, the windows showing a view of the city sprawling below them. The tension in the room was palpable. These were men who'd benefited from the mechanism for decades. These were men whose entire careers depended on the mechanism's continued operation.
Anjali had sources who'd provided her with transcripts. Not complete—the room had been secure, the conversations conducted in low voices—but enough to understand what was being said.
